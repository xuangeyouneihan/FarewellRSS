# FarewellRSS

> [简体中文](../../README.md) | English

> The layout of Luzhen's information hub was unlike that of any other place: by the street stood a great screen shaped like a carpenter's square, scrolling with news from every quarter, with a few chairs set before it for passers-by to rest. Working folk, knocking off at midday or dusk, would often spend a few minutes scrolling through the lively push notifications on the screen — that was some years ago; nowadays the recommendation algorithms have grown so clever that no scrolling is needed at all, the news arriving right before your eyes — and standing or sitting, they watched at their ease as they rested. If one cared to spend a little more effort, one could tap open the "interest tags" and sift out a few morsels suited to one's taste; and if one were willing to venture a dozen steps further, into the nook reserved for "deep reading," one could even dig out a few long articles. But these customers were mostly the short-coated crowd, who seldom had such leisure. Only those in long gowns would saunter into the elegant room behind the screen and, at an unhurried pace, fine-tune their subscription feeds.
>
> From the age of seventeen I worked as an assistant at the Xianheng Infoport at the edge of town. The manager said I was too slow-witted to wait upon the particular long-gowned patrons, so he set me to minding the trending ticker outside. The short-coated patrons out there, easygoing though they were, had grown fussy from being spoon-fed by the algorithm, and often grumbled that the feed pushed nothing but twice-chewed bread, and that one had to rummage for oneself to find anything fresh. They insisted on watching the information issue straight from the source, checking the timestamp with their own eyes and seeing for themselves it had not been tampered with, before they would rest easy: under such strict supervision, watering things down or passing off fakes was nearly impossible. So after a few days, the manager decided I was not up to the job either. Fortunately my recommender's face carried weight and I could not be dismissed, so I was transferred to the dull post of tending subscription feeds exclusively.
>
> From then on I stood all day behind that counter, in the cold and quiet. Though I made no mistakes, I found the work dreary, like staring at a blank wall. The manager wore a frosty face, and the patrons seldom brought any warmth either, which made it hard to keep one's spirits up; only when RSS came to the shop was there ever any amusement, and that is why I remember him to this day.
>
> RSS was the most conspicuous of those patrons who stood watching the screen yet insisted on wearing a long gown. He was a great hulking figure, his greyish-white face always bearing a few fresh scratches, with a tangled grizzled beard; and his long gown was dirty and tattered, as if it had not been laundered in ten years. When he spoke, it was all "feeds," "aggregation," "metadata," and the like — half unintelligible to the rest of us. The moment RSS entered the shop, everyone watching the screen turned to grin at him, and someone would call out: "RSS, there are new scratches on your face!" He paid no mind, but said to the counter: "Tune me two subscriptions, and a dish of trending tags." And he laid out nine clicks. Then they would deliberately bawl: "You must have been stealing other people's content again!" RSS would open his eyes wide: "How can you sully a man's good name out of thin air..." "What good name? The day before yesterday I saw with my own eyes how you scraped the Ho family's articles, and they chased you down and beat you." Then RSS would flush crimson, the veins on his brow standing out, and protest: "Aggregation cannot be counted as stealing... aggregation! ... The free flow of information — can that be called stealing?" Then would follow more incomprehensible talk, of "open protocols" and "decentralization," which set everyone roaring with laughter: inside and out, the shop filled with merry air.
>
> I heard it said behind his back that RSS had known glory in his day, much respected in the "golden age of blogging," but in the end he failed to ride the wave and would not curry favor; so he grew poorer and poorer, until hardly anyone used him anymore. Luckily he could write a fine line of code, so he built readers for people in exchange for a few meals. Unfortunately he had a certain quirk — he loved playing the teacher and was too lazy to accommodate anyone. Each time, a few days after setting up someone's reading rack, he would vanish without a trace, subscriber and subscription feeds alike. After this had happened several times, those who would hire him disappeared too. Left with no choice, RSS could not help but resort to a little furtive aggregation. But in our shop his conduct was better than most: he never defaulted — though he occasionally ran a tab, chalked up on the whitewashed board, within a month he would surely pay it off, and his name would be wiped away.
>
> When RSS had had his two feeds tuned, the ashen color would gradually leave his face, and someone would ask: "RSS, do you really know the 'truth'?" He would glance sidelong at the questioner with an air of disdaining to argue. They would press on: "Then how is it you can't produce even half a viral hit?" At once RSS would look crestfallen, a grey cast settling over his face, muttering about "filter bubbles" and "information cocoons" — none of which anyone understood. At this, the crowd would burst into laughter again: inside and out, the shop filled with merry air.
>
> At such times I could join in the laughter too, and the manager never scolded me. Indeed, whenever the manager saw RSS he would tease him in just this way, to general merriment. Knowing he could not talk with the others, RSS had to make do with me. Once he asked: "Have you ever used a subscription feed?" I nodded lazily. He said: "You have... then let me test you. How do you filter the 'news' in a newsfeed down to its clean version?" I thought, a thing about to be made obsolete — what right has he to test me? And I turned my face away. RSS waited a good while, then said earnestly: "You don't know? ... I'll teach you, remember it! These methods are worth remembering. When you're a manager someday, you'll need them for sifting information." I thought to myself that the manager and I were separated by a gulf of a hundred thousand miles, and besides, the manager never used such contraptions; amused and impatient, I answered shortly: "Who asked you to teach me? Isn't it just stripping out the ads and keeping the body text?" RSS looked delighted, tapping the edge of the screen with his long gaunt fingers, nodding: "Yes, yes! ... But there are four ways to sift the 'news' — do you know them?" More annoyed than ever, I pursed my lips and walked off. RSS was just about to dip his finger in the tea and sketch a diagram on the counter, but seeing my utter indifference, he sighed and looked deeply regretful.
>
> A few times, the children from next door heard the laughter and crowded round to watch the fun. RSS would deal them out a few "choice trivia feeds," one apiece. Having read them, the children still would not disperse, but stood gazing wistfully at his feed list. RSS grew flustered, spread his five fingers over the screen, bent down and said: "Not many left, I haven't many left." Then, straightening up, he would look at his feeds again and shake his head, saying to himself: "Not many, not many! Many, forsooth? Not many at all." And the children would scatter amid laughter.
>
> Thus RSS brought merriment to others, yet folk got along well enough without him.
>
> One day, two or three days before the Mid-Autumn Festival, the manager was slowly doing his accounts when he took down the whitewashed board and suddenly said: "RSS hasn't been in for a long time. He still owes nineteen clicks!" It was only then that I realized he had indeed not come for a long while. A patron said: "How could he come? ... His legs have been broken." The manager asked: "Oh?" "He was up to his old stealing again. This time, in a fit of folly, he stole his way into Big-V Ding's paywall. Is his content to be stolen?" "And then?" "Then? First he had to write a confession, then came the beating, which lasted half the night, and then his legs were broken." "And after that?" "After that his legs were broken." "Broken, and then what?" "What? ... Who knows? Perhaps he's dead." The manager asked no more, but bent his head and went on slowly working his abacus.
>
> After Mid-Autumn the autumn wind grew colder by the day, and the year's end drew near; I sat all day by the heating vent and had to wrap myself in a thin padded jacket. One afternoon, with not a single customer in the shop, I was dozing with my eyes closed when suddenly I heard: "Tune one subscription." The voice was very low, yet familiar. I looked up — no one in sight. Standing and peering out, I saw RSS sitting on the ground beneath the screen, facing the threshold. His face was dark and gaunt, a sorry sight; he wore a ragged lined jacket, his legs crossed beneath him, a cotton pad under them, slung from his shoulders with a straw rope. Seeing me, he said again: "Tune one subscription." The manager too craned out his head, saying as he did so: "Is that RSS? You still owe nineteen clicks!" RSS looked up dejectedly: "This... I'll settle it next time. This time it's cash on the barrel — I want a clean subscription." The manager, smiling as ever, said: "RSS, you've been stealing again!" But this time he did not much protest, only saying: "Don't mock me!" "Mock you? If you hadn't stolen, how would your legs have been broken?" RSS said in a low voice: "I fell... fell, fell..." His eyes seemed to beg the manager to let the matter rest. By now a few people had gathered, and they all laughed with the manager. I tuned one subscription and handed it over. From his tattered pocket he fished out four clicks and laid them in my palm, and I saw that his hands were caked with mud — he had crawled here on those hands. Presently, having finished reading that subscription, he slowly dragged himself away on those same hands, amid the talk and laughter of the others.
>
> After that, again RSS was not seen for a long time. At year's end the manager took down the whitewashed board and said: "RSS still owes nineteen clicks!" At the Dragon Boat Festival the next year he said it again: "RSS still owes nineteen clicks!" Come Mid-Autumn he did not say it, and by the next year's end he had still not been seen.
>
> To this day I have never seen him again — I suppose RSS really is dead.

## API

The backend communicates with the frontend entirely through the Google Reader API, with extended endpoints for registration, account deletion, TAG separation, and more. See [API.md](API.md) for details.

## Configuration

All configuration is done through `FAREWELL_RSS_*` environment variables (data directory, port, registration toggle, invite codes, fetch scheduling, etc.). OS environment variables take precedence over the `.env` file in the data directory. See [ENVIRONMENT.md](ENVIRONMENT.md) for details.

## Running

### Local

```powershell
cd frontend; pnpm install; pnpm build   # build the frontend (first time or after changes)
cd ..; farewell-rss                      # start backend and frontend with one command (port 3000 by default)
```

Open `http://localhost:3000` in your browser. For frontend development with hot reload, use `cd frontend; pnpm dev` (port 5173, proxied to 3000).

### Docker

```bash
docker build -t farewell-rss .
docker run -d -p 3000:3000 -v farewell-rss-data:/data farewell-rss
```

Or `docker compose up -d`. The image defaults to `FAREWELL_RSS_DATA_DIR=/data` (persisted via volume mount); override the other settings with environment variables (see above). Open `http://localhost:3000`.

## Roadmap

### Phase 1

> A basic RSS service.

- [X] ORM models and database infrastructure
- [X] Feed fetch engine (feedparser integration, incremental updates via ETag/Modified)
- [X] Repository layer (Feed, Entry, Enclosure, User, Subscription)
- [X] Service layer (subscription management, feed refresh scheduling)
- [X] FastAPI backend (Google Reader API + extended endpoints such as user registration)
- [X] Web frontend (subscription management, article reading)

### Phase 2

> In my view, RSS by itself cannot solve the information cocoon problem, because users only see what they have already subscribed to. And if they've subscribed to feeds they dislike, they won't necessarily want to read them either. So Phase 2 introduces recommendation and discovery features — through tag matching and community popularity signals, users can find content they might be interested in from feeds they haven't subscribed to, while a dual-slider with adjustable weights (personalization α + popularity β) hands control over "what to read" back to the user.

- [ ] User-contributed feed pool (new feeds auto-ingested + Wilson lower bound for cold start)
- [ ] Recommendation page including unsubscribed feeds, plus extended stream IDs for the Google Reader API
- [ ] User-adjustable recommendation algorithm (personalization weight α + popularity weight β)
- [ ] Web frontend as a PWA

### Phase 3

> One of RSS's great strengths is decentralization — anyone can publish an RSS feed. But if the discovery of RSS feeds remains in the hands of centralized instances, that strength counts for little; moreover, a single instance has a limited user base, and its feed pool is not large enough. So Phase 3 breaks down the walls between instances via P2P sync, merging the feed pools and popularity data accumulated by each instance into a community-built public resource, while defending against Sybil attacks with Wilson lower bound + instance trust weights, ensuring the data cannot be polluted by a handful of nodes.

- [ ] P2P synchronization of feed pools and popularity weights across instances
- [ ] Sybil attack defense via Wilson lower bound + instance trust weights
